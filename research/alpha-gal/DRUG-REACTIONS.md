# "Reacts to Multiple Medications" — What That Actually Means

**The second uncharacterized label in this case.** It has never been investigated, and it is very
unlikely to mean what it has been assumed to mean.

*August 2026. Research synthesis for discussion with treating clinicians. Not a diagnosis and not
medical advice.*

---

## The calibration point to open with

Her own file already proves the principle: **she has an IgE of 14.40 kU/L to pork, a food she has
never eaten and therefore has never reacted to.** A positive number is not a diagnosis. An
attribution that was never tested is not a finding.

That same discipline — reaction history first, test second, challenge to confirm — has never been
applied to her drug list. Ten years, and not one of those reactions has been characterized.

---

## Expected decomposition of the drug list

Stated as a **base-rate prior**, not a claim about her. The point is to set expectations before the
workup, not to pre-judge it.

| Category | Estimated share | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Not reproducible on blinded challenge** — coincidental symptoms, illness attributed to a drug, anticipatory response | **~50–65%** | Penicillin: >90% of labels are false; **96.5% de-labelled** on direct oral challenge |
| **Reproducible but pharmacologic, not immunologic** — COX-1 cross-reactive NSAID hypersensitivity, opioid and vancomycin direct histamine liberation, MRGPRX2 | **~20–35%** | Three of the four commonest implicated classes (penicillins, opiates, sulfonamides, NSAIDs) act by non-immunologic mechanisms |
| **Genuinely IgE-mediated to a drug or excipient** | **~5–10%** | <5% of penicillin labels are real; PEG allergy has ~37 case reports across four decades |
| **A mast cell disorder** (HaT, mastocytosis) | **~5% — i.e. the population base rate** | HaT is present in ~5.4% of healthy controls, two-thirds asymptomatic |

**The one-sentence version:** her food allergy is real immunology. Her drug list is most likely
**predominantly non-immunologic** — and the single most valuable output of an allergy consultation
may not be a new diagnosis but the **removal of several old ones**.

## The five mechanisms, and how to tell them apart

### 1. Cross-reactive NSAID hypersensitivity — the leading candidate, and the one that matters most

**She has chronic headache.** If she is a frequent analgesic user, NSAIDs are her highest-exposure
drug class by a wide margin — the one most likely to produce a repeating, genuinely reproducible
reaction pattern that then generalizes into "I react to everything."

The EAACI/ENDA classification (updated by WAO in 2025) sorts NSAID reactions into five phenotypes.
**Three are not immunologic at all:**

| Phenotype | Underlying disease | Presentation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **NERD / AERD** | Asthma ± nasal polyposis | Respiratory exacerbation after any NSAID |
| **NECD** | **Pre-existing chronic spontaneous urticaria** | Flare of the underlying urticaria |
| **NIUA** | None — otherwise healthy | Acute urticaria/angioedema. **The most frequent of the five** |

These are **COX-1 inhibition**, not allergy — which is exactly why patients react to chemically
unrelated NSAIDs. Reacting to aspirin *and* ibuprofen *and* naproxen is not three allergies. It is
**one pharmacologic mechanism**.

> ### ⭐ The highest-yield single question in this entire workup
>
> **Does she have, or has she ever had, chronic spontaneous urticaria?**
>
> If yes, "reacts to multiple medications" is very plausibly **NECD on a background urticarial
> diathesis** — a complete, mundane, non-immunologic explanation for reacting to structurally
> unrelated drugs. That should be excluded before anything exotic is entertained.

**And the consequence runs the opposite way to what patients are usually told:** cross-reactive NSAID
hypersensitivity typically leaves **selective COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib, etoricoxib) and low-dose
acetaminophen available after supervised challenge.** Many such patients are needlessly living with
no usable analgesic — which, in a woman with chronic headache, is not a small thing.

### 2. Direct histamine liberators — and the bedside tests that are free

**Opioids.** Morphine, codeine and meperidine are potent direct mast cell degranulators. The typical
picture is cutaneous — wheal along the injected vein, generalized itch, urticaria — **without**
hypotension or bronchospasm. **Fentanyl, oxycodone and hydromorphone are far weaker liberators.** So
the practical output of a "codeine allergy" label is usually: *she can have oxycodone.*

**Vancomycin flushing syndrome.** Rate-dependent by definition. **If slowing the infusion abolishes
it, it was never IgE.** That single observation de-labels the drug.

**Seven bedside discriminators favoring a direct liberator over IgE:**

1. Occurred on first-ever exposure
2. Confined to skin, no airway or cardiovascular involvement
3. **Abolished or attenuated by slowing the rate or reducing the dose**
4. Confined to the injection site or the infused vein
5. Fully prevented by antihistamine pre-treatment
6. Occurs to several structurally unrelated cationic drugs
7. Acute tryptase does not rise — though a flat tryptase does **not** exclude IgE in mild reactions

### 3. MRGPRX2 pseudo-allergy — plausible, fashionable, and currently unfalsifiable

A mast cell receptor that responds to cationic drugs with **no IgE and no prior sensitization**.
Classic agonists: **fluoroquinolones, vancomycin, neuromuscular blockers (not succinylcholine),
morphine, and peptide drugs like icatibant.**

**Be skeptical.** The reviews are blunt: evidence *"predominantly comes from animal models or in
vitro studies, and irrefutable clinical evidence in humans is still missing."* Most data are
transfected cell lines or the mouse orthologue MrgprB2. Calcium flux is the usual readout, and
calcium signals do not necessarily produce degranulation.

**Treat it as a hypothesis that predicts a specific drug list, not as a diagnosis.** If her list is
dominated by quinolones, vancomycin, opioids and perioperative agents, it is coherent. If it is
dominated by NSAIDs and oral tablets, it is not.

⚠️ **Tryptase cannot separate MRGPRX2 from IgE.** It distinguishes "mast cells degranulated" from
"they didn't." Nobody should tell her otherwise.

### 4. A hidden excipient — rare, but this is exactly the phenotype where it hides

The Sellaturay PEG-anaphylaxis series contains the sentence that justifies taking this seriously:

> *"Before diagnosis, **all 5 patients were mislabeled as allergic to multiple medications** and
> their clinical management had become increasingly challenging."*

That is her presenting phenotype exactly. PEG allergy is rare in absolute terms — but it is
**enriched** in the multi-drug-label population, because a hidden excipient is one of the very few
mechanisms that genuinely does cause reactions to unrelated drugs.

**Culprits worth knowing:** PEG/macrogol (molecular-weight dependent — she could react to PEG 3350
and tolerate low-MW PEG) · polysorbate 80 (partial, not universal, cross-reactivity with PEG) ·
carboxymethylcellulose (injectable steroid suspensions) · gelatin · carmine/cochineal.

**One to actively de-escalate:** **tartrazine (FD&C Yellow #5) was largely exonerated by
double-blind placebo-controlled challenge.** It gets blamed constantly and the negative evidence is
ignored.

> ### ⭐ Excipient triangulation — costs nothing, requires no needle, almost never done
>
> Pull the full inactive-ingredient list from the product monograph of **every implicated product and
> every tolerated product**, and intersect them.
>
> If PEG 3350 appears in all five reactions and none of the tolerated drugs, you have a hypothesis
> worth skin testing. **If nothing intersects, excipients are unlikely and testing will only generate
> false positives.**

⚠️ **Do not screen blind.** In one COVID-vaccine cohort, **16 of 126 interpretable PEG/polysorbate
skin tests (12.7%) were positive** — far above any plausible true prevalence. A positive PEG skin
test in a low-prior patient is more likely a false positive than a diagnosis, and would add a sixth
wrong label to a chart that already has too many.

### 5. Hereditary alpha-tryptasemia — do the arithmetic before ordering anything

**~5.4% of healthy controls carry it. Up to two-thirds of carriers have no related symptoms.**

Roughly 1 in 20 people in any waiting room has HaT, and most of them are fine. **A finding present in
5% of everyone and silent in most who carry it has very low specificity as an explanation for
anything.** Finding it in her would be about as informative as finding she has blue eyes.

The study designed to test this is **Chollet & Akin, *JACI* 2022 — "Hereditary alpha tryptasemia is
not associated with specific clinical phenotypes."** They used a **random biorepository population**
rather than a referral cohort. The prior symptom-association literature came almost entirely from
tertiary mast cell clinics — genotype only people who came in complaining of flushing, GI symptoms
and drug reactions, find HaT in 5% of them *because it is in 5% of everyone*, and you will
"discover" an association that is pure ascertainment bias.

**What this means practically:**

- ✅ **Draw the baseline tryptase.** ~$50, ten years overdue, and it closes or opens the entire mast
  cell branch of this workup.
- ✅ **A baseline <8 ng/mL effectively rules HaT out.** Do **not** order TPSAB1 genotyping in that
  case — HaT is the commonest cause of an elevated baseline, and a normal baseline is the rule-out.
- ⚠️ **A positive result changes almost nothing.** No HaT-specific therapy. Doesn't change which
  drugs she avoids, whether she carries epinephrine, or any challenge protocol. What it delivers is
  explanation — which has real value for someone told for ten years she has an untested "histamine
  problem," but also carries a real cost: **a permanent genetic label that can justify continued
  avoidance of drugs she could safely take.**

**In a case whose central problem is an excess of unexamined labels, adding a low-specificity genetic
one before doing a single functional test is the wrong order of operations.**

⚠️ And note: **an elevated tryptase would not confirm a "histamine problem" either.** Tryptase is a
mast cell marker; it is not histamine. If anyone proposes a serum DAO or histamine assay to settle
the question — that is a dead end and is not a validated diagnostic.

## ⭐ The mammalian-excipient list is probably wrong in both directions

**This section corrects an over-warning in an earlier draft of `toolkit.md`.**

Her sensitization is to **mammalian serum albumins** (Fel d 2 → Sus s 1, Bos d 6). She is
**alpha-gal negative**. Those are different targets implicating different products, and **most
alpha-gal medication guidance circulating online does not apply to her.**

**The rule that does most of the work:** *serum albumin is a blood protein. To carry albumin, an
excipient must be a crude protein or tissue extract. Purified carbohydrates, purified lipids and
purified structural proteins do not carry it.*

| Excipient | Verdict for her | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gelatin capsule shells** | ❌ **Effectively a non-issue** | Gelatin is **denatured type I collagen** — a purified structural protein. The documented gelatin allergens are the collagen α1/α2 chains, a different protein with no meaningful homology to albumin. The other gelatin risk is alpha-gal, and **she is alpha-gal negative.** *(Even in true AGS, <10% react to gelatin in food.)* **She does not need to avoid capsules.** |
| **Magnesium stearate** | ❌ **Effectively a non-issue** | A saponified purified **lipid**. Proteins are removed in processing. One case report of urticaria exists; the theoretical concern is alpha-gal on glycolipids, which does not apply to her |
| **Bovine-derived glycerin** | ❌ **Non-issue** | A three-carbon polyol. No protein of any kind. Should not appear on any avoidance list |
| **Human serum albumin** (in MMR, IVIG, some biologics) | ❌ **Non-issue** | It is albumin — but **human**. Self-protein, no meaningful cross-reactivity. Worth pre-empting, because it confuses people |
| **Heparin / LMWH** | 🟡 Low, theoretical | Highly purified glycosaminoglycan, very low residual protein. **Do not withhold anticoagulation over this** |
| **Lactose as a filler or inhaler carrier** | 🟡 **The one genuinely worth discussing** | Routinely carries residual milk proteins — found in **all** lactose-containing dry powder inhalers tested, and dairy-derived excipients appear in **97% of inhaled anti-asthmatics** and **69% of film-coated NSAID tablets**. Bos d 6 *is* a minor milk protein, so trace carriage is plausible. But documented reactors are **cow's-milk-protein allergic**, which she is not established to be, and inhaled exposure carries the real risk, not oral tablets. **Action: check milk sIgE and Bos d 6 — do not blanket-avoid lactose** |
| **Residual bovine serum albumin** in cell-culture-derived biologics | 🟡 Low but non-zero — **and the theoretically correct concern for her** | Genuine trace Bos d 6 as a monitored manufacturing impurity. Worth naming to the allergist, in contrast to the gelatin concern she has probably been warned about |
| **Porcine pancreatin / pancrelipase** (Creon, Zenpep, Pertzye) | 🔴 **HIGHEST CONCERN — the one to actually flag** | A **crude porcine pancreatic tissue extract** — an unpurified mixture of tissue proteins from blood-perfused tissue. **Porcine serum albumin contamination is expected, not theoretical.** If pancreatin is ever proposed — and it might be, in a GI workup — it warrants pre-emptive discussion and possibly skin testing with the product |
| **Perioperative bovine/porcine products** — gelatin sponges, bovine thrombin, fibrin sealants (bovine aprotinin), gelatin colloids | 🔴 Real, but belongs on a **pre-anesthetic plan**, not a daily avoidance list | Some are crude bovine protein preparations. Aprotinin and bovine thrombin are genuine bovine protein exposures |

**Summary: over-warned on gelatin capsules, magnesium stearate and glycerin. Probably silent on
porcine pancreatin and residual BSA — the two that are mechanistically real.**

## The workup, in order

### Phase 0 — Chart archaeology. Do this before any test.

The highest-yield step and the cheapest. **One table, one row per reaction:**

```
Drug | Dose & route | Brand/formulation | Time from dose to onset |
OBJECTIVE signs (rash seen? BP? O2 sat? treatment given?) | Treated with what |
Anything from that class tolerated SINCE? | Concurrent illness at the time?
```

Three things to extract:

- **Objective vs. subjective.** Documented urticaria, hypotension or desaturation is a different
  object from remembered nausea or "feeling wrong."
- **Tolerated exposures.** Almost every multi-drug-label patient has since taken an implicated drug
  uneventfully. Those instances are diagnostic **and are never recorded.**
- **The excipient intersection**, as above.

### Phase 1 — The baseline that has never existed

**Baseline serum tryptase — ideally ×2**, given documented baseline variability · **total IgE** ·
**CBC with differential** · component testing: **Fel d 2 (e220), Sus s 1 (e222), Bos d 6 (e204),
Fel d 1** · **cow's milk protein sIgE** (settles the lactose question directly) · and an explicit
**chronic spontaneous urticaria history**.

### Phase 2 — Only if Phase 1 is abnormal

Elevated baseline tryptase → repeat to confirm → **TPSAB1 copy number by ddPCR**, and consider a
mastocytosis evaluation. **Normal baseline (<8) → stop. Do not order the genetic test.**

### ⭐ Phase 3 — The standing order that should have existed for ten years

**Acute tryptase must exceed:**

> **1.2 × (her own baseline) + 2 ng/mL** — the consensus "20% + 2" rule

- **Acute sample: within 4 hours of the event** (peak ~1–2 h)
- **Baseline sample: ≥24 hours after**

**Ten years of reactions have generated zero acute tryptase measurements.** That is the single
reason this is still uncharacterized. **She should be given a standing lab order and a wallet card
with the 4-hour window written on it**, so any ED she presents to draws it.

Two caveats to attach: the formula establishes **that** mast cells degranulated, not **why** — it
cannot separate IgE from MRGPRX2. And a flat tryptase does not exclude a reaction, especially a mild
or skin-limited one.

### Phase 4 — Where drug testing is valid, and where it misleads

**Valid:** beta-lactams, neuromuscular blockers, platinum agents, heparins, iodinated contrast.

⚠️ **Unreliable or actively misleading:** **opioids, fluoroquinolones, vancomycin** — these have
direct mast-cell-activating properties and raise wheals in *normal controls* at high concentration.
Only validated non-irritating concentrations are interpretable, and even then specificity is poor.
**PEG/polysorbate** — 12.7% positivity in screening, utility openly debated, and the skin test itself
has caused systemic reactions.

**Drug provocation testing is the gold standard** and the only thing that resolves this picture. In
this population it must be **single-blind and placebo-controlled** — an open challenge in a patient
with a multi-drug label is not interpretable.

### Phase 5 — De-labeling, highest yield first

1. **NSAIDs — phenotype, then challenge to a COX-2 inhibitor and low-dose acetaminophen.** Given
   chronic headache, **this is arguably her highest quality-of-life priority**, ahead of everything
   else here. Restoring a usable analgesic changes her daily life.
2. **Penicillin / beta-lactams.** Risk-stratify (PEN-FAST); low-risk goes to **direct oral challenge
   without skin testing**. The evidence: **96.5% de-labelled, no serious adverse reactions or
   anaphylaxis across the systematic review**, 2.3% minor immediate reactions. The best-evidenced,
   safest, highest-payoff intervention available to her.
3. **Opioids** — if the label is codeine or morphine with a skin-limited reaction, the answer is
   **substitution to oxycodone or fentanyl**, not a challenge.
4. **Vancomycin** — if the history is infusion-rate flushing, re-administer slowly. It de-labels
   itself.
5. **Excipients** — only if Phase 0 triangulation found a real intersection.

### Phase 6 — Replace the list with a document

The chart currently holds **a list of nouns**. What she needs is **a list of decisions**: what is
confirmed, what is excluded by challenge, what is untested, what is safe to give, and a
pre-anesthetic plan (NMBAs, opioids, gelatin colloids, chlorhexidine, latex).

## ⚠️ Why the label itself is doing her harm

The penicillin literature is the cleanest evidence that a wrong allergy label is not neutral:

- Labels are associated with **worse clinical outcomes, increased in-hospital mortality, and longer
  hospital stays**
- They drive second-line agents that are **less effective, more toxic and costlier**
- After de-labeling, patients were **13× more likely to receive penicillin**, **22% less likely to
  get restricted antibiotics**, and **27% less likely to get broad-spectrum antibiotics**

For her specifically, the label has already cost ten years without a workup, and may currently be
costing her any usable analgesic for chronic headache. **The label is doing her more harm than the
drugs.**

### Multiple Drug Intolerance Syndrome — the epidemiology, and how to handle it

**MDIS** — reactions to ≥3 drug classes without a demonstrable immunologic mechanism — has a
prevalence of **6%** in one large US health system and **pooled 10%** in meta-analysis. Risk factors:
**female sex in every included study**, older age, GI disorders, high healthcare utilization, and
anxiety or depression.

**How to raise this without losing the room:**

1. **Lead with what is confirmed.** She has a real, laboratory-confirmed, immunologically coherent
   food allergy. Say it first and without hedging.
2. **Attack the label, not the experience.** "Something happened to you each of those times" and
   "the label on your chart is wrong" are compatible statements. The reaction was real; the *causal
   attribution* was never tested.
3. ⭐ **Use her own file as the illustration.** She has IgE 14.40 to pork and has never eaten pork.
   That is the clearest possible demonstration, **from her own results**, that a positive number is
   not a diagnosis. It reframes skepticism as rigor applied consistently, not doubt aimed at her.
4. **De-labeling is subtractive, and that is the selling point.** Every drug returned is a drug she
   has if she needs it. **This is a workup that gives things back.**
5. **Do not lead with anxiety or depression.** The association is epidemiologically real and it is
   also the fastest way to lose the room. It is a fact about a population, not a claim about her.
   What it should change is **method** — blinded, placebo-controlled challenges rather than open ones
   — not tone.

## Do NOT

- **Serum DAO or histamine assays** for "histamine intolerance" — not validated diagnostics
- **Blanket avoidance of gelatin capsules, magnesium stearate or glycerin** on albumin grounds
- **Genetic testing before a baseline tryptase**
- **Open (unblinded) drug challenges** in this population
- **Any test whose positive result would not change a decision**
