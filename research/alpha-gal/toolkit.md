# Alpha-Gal Practical Toolkit

Three working documents built from the dossier: a medication and product audit, a
procedure-safety card, and a symptom diary designed around the delayed reaction.

Print or copy these. They are meant to be used, not read.

---

# 1. Medication and Product Audit

**Why this first:** she reacts to medications, which means an unrecognized daily exposure is plausible.
This is the highest-yield hour available and it costs nothing. Work through every bottle, box and
supplement in the house — prescription, over-the-counter, vitamin, and supplement alike.

## Tier 1 — Stop and check these today

These are documented alpha-gal-containing products. If any appear on her list, they go to the
prescriber **before** the next dose. Do not stop a prescribed medication unilaterally — several of
these treat conditions where abrupt discontinuation is dangerous.

| Product | Why | Substitute to ask about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conjugated equine estrogens** — Premarin, Prempro, Duavee | Derived from pregnant mare urine. A **daily** mammalian exposure that never appears in a food diary | Synthetic or plant-derived estradiol |
| **Desiccated thyroid** — Armour Thyroid, NP Thyroid, Nature-Throid, WP Thyroid | Porcine thyroid gland. **Thyroglobulin tested as the most potent alpha-gal allergen measured** — active below 10 pg/mL in every patient tested | Synthetic levothyroxine |
| **Gelatin capsule shells** — any medication or supplement in a soft or hard gelatin capsule | Bovine/porcine collagen. Called the excipient of greatest concern, and she is gelatin-reactive | HPMC (plant) capsules, tablets, or compounded alternative |
| **Pancreatic enzymes** — Creon, Zenpep, Viokase, Pertzye, Pancreatin | Porcine pancreas, among the most potent products tested. **Reactivity is brand-specific** | No synthetic alternative — needs allergist-supervised testing, not blanket avoidance |
| **Heparin / enoxaparin** | Porcine intestinal mucosa. See the procedure card below | Argatroban, bivalirudin — a decision for the treating team |
| **Glucosamine / chondroitin** | Chondroitin is typically bovine or shark cartilage | Discontinue if non-essential |
| **Collagen supplements, bone broth powders** | Bovine or porcine collagen by definition | Marine (fish) collagen |

## Tier 2 — Worth reviewing if symptoms persist

Lower-probability, but reasonable to examine once Tier 1 is clear.

- **Vaccines containing gelatin** — MMR, varicella, **Zostavax** (live zoster), yellow fever, oral
  typhoid, FluMist, some Tdap. **Shingrix is gelatin-free** and the standard alternative for zoster.
  Evidence here is genuinely conflicting: basophil testing is positive, but uneventful administrations
  are also documented. **Do not blanket-defer vaccination** — make it an individualized decision, and
  consider observed administration. **Never withhold rabies vaccine after a possible exposure.**
- **Carrageenan** — disputed, but a legitimate targeted elimination trial *specifically* for someone
  with unexplained medication reactions. Found in some acetaminophen, omeprazole and dabigatran
  formulations, chocolate milk, deli turkey, plant milks, tofu.
- **Lactose** in tablets, **glycerin**, **mono- and diglycerides**, **stearic acid**.

## Tier 3 — On patient lists, but the evidence does not support routine avoidance

Included so time is not wasted here. Over-restriction has real costs and distracts from what matters.

- **Magnesium stearate** — not structurally similar to alpha-gal; major commercial sources have been
  plant-based (coconut, palm) since at least 2022. Provider guidance: "little to no alpha-gal."
- **Personal care products** — lanolin, tallow soaps, collagen skincare. **No published study shows
  transdermal absorption sufficient to cause systemic reactions.** Advocacy guidance itself says most
  patients tolerate lanolin. Reserve for genuinely refractory cases after diet and medications clear.

## How to actually check an unknown product

1. **DailyMed** (dailymed.nlm.nih.gov) — search the drug, read the full inactive ingredient list.
2. **Call the manufacturer** and ask specifically: *"Are any active or inactive ingredients in this
   product derived from mammalian sources?"* Note that in one analysis **40% of pharmaceutical
   medical-information departments could not accurately answer this** — so a "no" from a call center
   is weak evidence.
3. **Ask the pharmacist to run a formal review**, including capsule shells. The Institute for Safe
   Medication Practices has published guidance instructing pharmacists to evaluate both active and
   inactive ingredients for alpha-gal patients — cite it if you meet resistance.

# 2. Procedure and Emergency Safety Card

**Purpose:** the realistic worst-case outcomes in alpha-gal syndrome are medical procedures, not meals.
Give a copy to every treating clinician, keep one in her wallet, and photograph it on her phone.

> ## ⚕️ ALPHA-GAL SYNDROME — CLINICAL ALERT
>
> **Patient has IgE-mediated allergy to galactose-α-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal).**
> This is a **carbohydrate** allergy to non-primate mammalian products.
> **Reactions to ingested allergen are DELAYED 3–6 hours. Reactions to IV/parenteral exposure are
> IMMEDIATE.**
>
> ### Agents to avoid — request substitution
>
> | Avoid | Substitute |
> | --- | --- |
> | **Heparin (porcine), LMWH** | Argatroban, bivalirudin |
> | **Gelatin hemostatics** — Gelfoam, Surgifoam, Surgiflo, FloSeal | **Oxidized regenerated cellulose (Surgicel)**, plant polysaccharide hemostats, **human recombinant thrombin** |
> | **Gelatin colloids** — Gelofusine, Gelaspan, Haemaccel | Crystalloid or albumin |
> | **Bovine/porcine valves, pericardial patches, xenograft bone** | Discuss mechanical or synthetic options |
> | **Collagen dressings, dural substitutes, catgut suture, biologic mesh** | Synthetic sealants, synthetic sutures |
> | **Gelatin capsules** in discharge prescriptions | HPMC capsules or tablets |
> | **Glycerin-containing propofol formulations**, lidocaine patches | Formulation-specific — ask pharmacy |
> | **Cetuximab** | Contraindicated |
>
> ### Transfusion
>
> **Patient blood group: ____________**
> If group **O**: avoid group **B and AB plasma and platelets** where feasible. Transfusion-Related
> Alpha-Gal Syndrome (TRAGS) is documented, including one fatality.
>
> ### Before any planned procedure
>
> - Pre-operative **alpha-gal IgE level** — titer correlates with heparin reaction risk
>   (median 75 IU/mL in reactors vs 8 in non-reactors)
> - Consider **premedication**; for cardiac surgery ask about **heparin desensitization protocols**
> - **Pharmacist review of every intraoperative agent and every discharge prescription**
>
> ### If a reaction occurs
>
> **EPINEPHRINE FIRST — IM anterolateral thigh. Antihistamines do not treat anaphylaxis.**
> Observe **4–12 hours.** ⚠️ **The observation clock runs from SYMPTOM ONSET, not from ingestion.**
> A patient presenting 5 hours after a meal is at peak allergen presentation, not past it — **do not
> shorten observation because the meal was hours ago.**
>
> ### GI symptoms are anaphylaxis in this patient
>
> Her phenotype is **GI-predominant**. Severe abdominal pain, repetitive vomiting, or faintness are
> **epinephrine-level symptoms** and may occur **without hives or wheeze.** Standard food-allergy
> criteria will under-treat her.
>

# 3. Symptom and Exposure Diary

**Why this specific design:** with a 3–6 hour delay, memory is useless and ordinary food diaries fail.
The two timestamps are the entire point — they let you compute her personal latency, which no one
currently knows. For a GI-predominant patient this is the single most useful diagnostic instrument
available, and it costs nothing.

Keep it for **at least four weeks** before drawing conclusions, and bring it to the appointment.

```
DATE: __________

── INTAKE ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Time            What was eaten / taken (be specific — brand, fat content)
______    ____________________________________________________________
______    ____________________________________________________________
______    ____________________________________________________________

Any medication, supplement, or new product taken today?
______________________________________________________________________

── COFACTORS (circle any present) ────────────────────────────────────
  alcohol    vigorous exercise    NSAIDs (ibuprofen/aspirin/naproxen)
  illness / infection    poor sleep    high stress    menstruation
  hot bath / sauna       high-fat meal

── SYMPTOMS ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Onset time: ______        →  LATENCY (onset − intake): ______ hours

Symptoms (circle):  abdominal pain   nausea   vomiting   diarrhea
                    headache   flushing   itching   hives   swelling
                    faintness / dizziness   throat tightness   wheeze

Severity:  [ ] 1 mild   [ ] 2   [ ] 3 moderate   [ ] 4   [ ] 5 severe
Duration: ______        Treatment taken: ______________________________
Epinephrine used?  [ ] yes  [ ] no

── NOTES ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
______________________________________________________________________
```

### What to look for after four weeks

- **Her median latency.** If it clusters at 3–6 hours, that is classic alpha-gal. Shorter latencies
  do not rule it out — organ meats and parenteral exposures react faster.
- **Cofactor patterns.** If reactions cluster on days with alcohol, exercise or NSAIDs, that is the
  most modifiable finding in the whole diary and it is often the difference between a tolerated meal
  and a reaction.
- **Symptom days with no identifiable mammalian intake.** These are the important ones. A high count
  points toward the mast cell hyperactivity component rather than dietary alpha-gal — which changes
  the treatment from "avoid harder" to "treat the mast cells."
- **Whether headache tracks with the GI symptoms or runs independently.** Migraine is five times more
  common in the mast-cell-hyperactivity subgroup, and this is worth knowing.

## A note on how to use all of this

The dossier is the reasoning; this is the execution. If only one thing gets done, make it
**Tier 1 of the medication audit** — it is free, takes an hour, requires no appointment, and it is the
most likely place a fixable daily exposure is hiding.

*Prepared as a research summary for discussion with treating clinicians. Not medical advice. Nothing
here should be started, stopped, or changed without her physician.*
