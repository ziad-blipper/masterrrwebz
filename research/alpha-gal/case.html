Diagnostic reconstruction · August 2026

The wrong antibody

A decade of illness, a year of treatment, and a diagnosis that her own bloodwork never supported. Here is what the evidence actually shows.

Not a diagnosis, and not medical advice

Safety first

These reactions meet the criteria for anaphylaxis

Before any diagnostic question: her reaction pattern crosses the threshold that defines anaphylaxis under the standard US criteria, and she does not appear to be carrying epinephrine.

The NIAID/FAAN definition requires two organ systems. Hers involves three.

Two auto-injectors, and why it isn't close

Specific IgE level predicts whether someone will react — not how badly. There is no reliable severity biomarker in food allergy.

Which means a decade of moderate reactions offers no assurance that the next one won't be severe. Severity varies episode to episode with dose and cofactors. Steak with wine after a workout is a materially different exposure than steak alone.

Flag her records for surgery. There is a published case of this syndrome discovered only when a patient anaphylaxed on the operating table — to a bovine serum albumin tissue adhesive. Bovine and porcine hemostatics, gelatin colloids, heparin and bioprosthetic valves all matter here.

Sensitization requires exposure. An antibody of 14.40 to a meat she has never consumed cannot have arisen in her gut. It came from somewhere else — and there is only one plausible route.

She had cats for most of her life. Cat serum albumin is 82% identical to pork albumin. The antibody was raised against the cat and binds the pig.

02 — The evidence

Her serology reproduces the homology ladder exactly

Cross-reactivity between albumins tracks how similar they are to the original sensitizer. If one cat-driven antibody explains all three of her meat results, they should fall in the same order as their sequence identity to cat albumin.

Perfect rank correlation. That is the signature of a single cross-reactive sensitizer. It is not what three independent food allergies look like — and alpha-gal, a sugar shared evenly across mammalian meats, produces no gradient of this shape at all.

The clinical picture fits too

Her reactions begin immediately, opening with itchy mouth and throat before progressing to dizziness and nausea. Oral itching during the meal is the described opening symptom of albumin-mediated meat allergy — proteins contact mast cells in the mouth while chewing.

And her two trigger foods are steak and lamb: the two mammalian meats habitually served pink. Albumin begins denaturing above 60 °C; a medium-rare steak reaches only 52–57 °C internally. That is not a coincidence in her history — it is the mechanism showing itself.

03 — Exclusion

Why it is not alpha-gal syndrome

The original diagnosis was reasonable to consider — she had a meat allergy in tick country, and GI-predominant alpha-gal is a real, under-recognized phenotype. The error was keeping the label after the confirmatory test came back undetectable.

Two findings close it

Oral itching. Fewer than 1% of alpha-gal patients experience it. It is her leading symptom. This follows necessarily from the biology: alpha-gal rides on glycolipids requiring digestion and lymphatic transport — which is why the reaction is delayed. There is no free alpha-gal at the mouth during a meal.

And the arithmetic

Alpha-gal is the dominant epitope on mammalian meat. The alpha-gal assay measures that epitope directly, on a carrier protein denser in alpha-gal than any meat extract. If her meat IgE were alpha-gal-directed, that assay would read high.

It reads undetectable while pork sits at 14.40. Her meat antibodies are aimed at proteins, not the sugar.

Verdict: excluded, under 5% residual probability. Worth closing formally with a total IgE and one repeat — not because it will be positive, but because a diagnosis closed properly stays closed.

04 — Correction

"Just cook it well done" is not a safety plan

Heat-lability is real, and it likely explains why steak is her trigger. That makes it a valuable diagnostic clue. It does not make it a rule she can live by.

What the controlled data show

In the one double-blind placebo-controlled study to test this, heating reduced but did not abolish reactivity: 4 of 10 patients still reacted to heated bovine albumin. Only industrially sterilized preparations gave uniformly negative challenges.

Beef also carries genuinely heat-stable allergens — myoglobin, Bos d 13, and six protein fractions that survive 85 °C for two hours.

A ~40% failure rate, unverifiable restaurant doneness, cofactor stacking, and a cardiovascular symptom in her history make this the wrong candidate for a rule of thumb. If it proves true it could return real food to her diet — but that finding belongs to a supervised challenge, not the kitchen table.

05 — Honesty

The counterweight

A 2021 cohort followed 15 cat-allergic patients with albumin sensitization. Seven carried bovine albumin antibodies. All of them ate beef, pork and milk with no symptoms at all.

Albumin sensitization is common; albumin allergy is much rarer. This is the strongest argument against the leading hypothesis, and it belongs in the record rather than smoothed over.

What separates her from that cohort is a decade of reproducible, stereotyped, multi-system reactions. Her diagnosis rests on that history. Component testing confirms the mechanism.

One consequence worth carrying forward: if milk or bovine albumin IgE comes back positive later, do not automatically call it a dairy allergy. In a cat-sensitized patient that is frequently a silent cross-reaction — and diagnosing on serology alone would repeat the exact error that produced the alpha-gal label.

06 — Discipline

What this does not explain

The greatest risk now is replacing one over-unifying label with another.

Albumin allergy accounts for her meat allergy. It does not account for her illness:

  • Ten years of GI-predominant symptoms — which did not improve after a full year of complete mammalian avoidance. That non-response is itself evidence that meat is not the driver
  • Chronic headache, distinct from the migraine that ends an acute reaction
  • Bread-associated joint pain and inflammation
  • Reactions to multiple unrelated medications
  • The "serious histamine problem"
  • Gelatin reactivity — a genuine discordance. Gelatin is denatured collagen and contains no albumin at all
The candidate that would tie it together

Untreated celiac disease would account for the GI symptoms, the headache, the bread-associated joint pain — and the histamine problem itself. Diamine oxidase, the enzyme that degrades histamine, is a brush-border enzyme made by the small intestinal lining that celiac destroys.

Adult onset after a lifetime of eating bread is the norm, not the exception — mean diagnosis age is 46–56, and roughly a quarter are diagnosed after 60.

⏳ This one has a clock on it

tTG-IgA falls by roughly half within 30–60 days off gluten. Test before any gluten-free trial — a few well-intentioned weeks can turn diagnosable celiac into undiagnosable celiac.

And total serum IgA must be drawn on the same specimen. IgA deficiency runs 2–3% in celiac — ten to fifteen times background — and makes a negative result uninterpretable. That is the likeliest reason a prior "not celiac" was wrong.

07 — Tests

What to order

  1. 01

    Fel d 2 — cat serum albumin

    The decisive test. Should be her highest value if the reconstruction holds. Order it directly, not through a reflex panel — see the warning below.

  2. 02

    Bos d 6 — bovine serum albumin

    The direction of the gradient settles the mechanism. Cat albumin higher means the cat was the sensitizer; bovine higher means a milk-bridge allergy instead.

  3. 03

    tTG-IgA + total serum IgA — while still eating gluten

    Both on the same draw. The total IgA is non-negotiable. Add HLA-DQ2/DQ8, which is diet-independent and rules celiac out permanently if negative.

  4. 04

    Total serum IgE

    Never drawn. Every specific IgE above is uninterpretable without the denominator.

  5. 05

    Baseline serum tryptase

    Her histamine problem has never been objectively measured. If ≥8 ng/mL, follow with TPSAB1 copy number for hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.

  6. 06

    Repeat alpha-gal, alongside the total IgE

    Formal closure of a label she has carried for a year.

⚠️ An ordering trap that would waste the most important test

Combined cat panels typically run components only if whole-cat IgE is ≥0.35. She has been cat-free for 3–4 years and that value may well have fallen — which would silently cancel the Fel d 2 result. Order Fel d 2 as a standalone component.

Pending results, avoid

All mammalian meat — including pork. Her pork antibody is her highest value and has never been clinically tested. "She's never had a problem with pork" must not become "she should try pork." Especially avoid anything rare or medium, and cured, dried or smoked products, which are documented as more reactive than well-cooked meat.

No self-directed home challenge of any kind. Not a small bite of well-done steak to see.

What to tell her. She does not have alpha-gal syndrome — the test that confirms it was negative twice, and her reaction pattern does not fit the disease. She does have a real IgE-mediated food allergy, and her reactions have met the criteria for anaphylaxis, which makes this more serious than the label she was given, not less. The likely cause is a protein shared between cats and mammalian meat, and her cats are the probable original source.

And some of her symptoms have a separate cause. Solving the meat allergy will not necessarily solve the chronic illness — and assuming one diagnosis explains everything is precisely the error that produced the alpha-gal label in the first place.
