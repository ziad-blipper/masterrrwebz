Prepared August 2026 · For discussion with her doctors

What to do next

The alpha-gal diagnosis does not hold up. Her blood test for it was negative twice, a year apart. She does have a real food allergy — but it is something else. And underneath it sits a separate ten-year illness that has never actually been investigated.

Start here

Two things that cannot wait

Everything else on this page is discussion. These two are actions. Both are cheap. Neither depends on figuring out anything else first.

1

Get an epinephrine prescription. Two auto-injectors.

Her reactions hit three body systems at once — itchy mouth and throat, dizziness, nausea. That meets the definition of anaphylaxis. She has none.

Blood test numbers do not predict how bad a reaction will be. Ten years of moderate reactions tell you nothing about the next one.

And there is a specific risk here. She was told that three rounds of laser acupuncture cured her gluten allergy. If she or that practitioner decides the same thing could clear her meat allergy, she will eat steak to prove it — with no epinephrine in the house. That is a predictable path to a severe reaction.

2

Get the celiac blood test before she stops eating bread.

This is the only test here with a deadline. The antibodies it looks for disappear within weeks of going gluten-free. Once they are gone, the only way back is eating gluten daily for six to eight weeks on purpose — which most people refuse.

Ask for tTG-IgA and total serum IgA, together. The second one is not optional: about 1 in 500 people cannot make the antibody the first test measures, and in celiac patients it is 10–15 times more common. Without it, a negative result means nothing.

The one instruction that matters most

Do not change her diet until that blood test comes back. The instinct after reading this will be to cut bread immediately. That would destroy the ability to diagnose the thing it may be reacting to. Two weeks of patience protects an answer that lasts a lifetime.

The picture

She has two problems, not one

This is the whole story. One wrong label got stretched to cover everything, and so nothing underneath it was ever looked at.

Problem 1 · The food allergy

Real, immediate, and IgE-driven. Minutes after eating, not hours. It starts in her mouth.

We have a good idea what it is and one blood draw settles it.

Problem 2 · The ten-year illness

Stomach pain, nausea, loose stool, headaches, joint pain. Came on slowly, no clear start.

Never investigated. No scope, no celiac test, no stool test, no basic bloods.

Why this matters

A full year of cutting out all meat and dairy did nothing for the second problem. That is not a failure — it is evidence. It tells you the chronic illness is not being driven by the foods she removed. Something else is going on, and nobody has looked for it.

Problem 1

What the meat allergy probably is

Her numbers: pork 14.40 · lamb 10.20 · beef 2.38 · alpha-gal <0.10 (undetectable).

The strange part: pork is her highest number and she has never eaten pork. Her lowest number, beef, is the food she actually reacts to. That pattern is the signature of a shared protein — one allergy showing up across several meats — not three separate allergies.

The leading suspect is serum albumin, a blood protein shared by cats and farm animals. She had cats for decades and reacts to them. This is a known syndrome.

The mistake that would waste the whole test

Order Fel d 2 on its own — Quest test code 93382. Do not let it be ordered as part of a "cat allergy panel." Those panels only run the piece we need if her general cat number is above a cutoff, and she has been cat-free for three or four years. Ordered the wrong way, the single most important test in her workup silently never runs and comes back looking like a clean negative.

The most likely truth

It is probably not one elegant answer. More likely three ordinary things at once — say, an IBS-type disorder plus migraine plus low iron. Less satisfying, considerably more likely to be true, and each part is treatable on its own.

Take this to the appointment

The blood draw — about $150

All of it in one sitting. This replaces the sprawling, expensive test list we first wrote. She has none of the warning signs that would justify going bigger straight away.

  • tTG-IgA + total serum IgA — celiac. While still eating gluten.
  • CBC + ferritin + CRP — iron. The CRP has to go with it, or the ferritin can read falsely normal.
  • Baseline tryptase — the "histamine problem" she was labelled with, finally measured. Ten years overdue.
  • Total IgE — without this, none of her meat numbers can be read properly.
  • Fel d 2 (Quest 93382), Bos d 6 (Quest 2612), gelatin c74 (Quest 35352) — settles the allergy.
  • TSH, B12, folate, vitamin D — cheap, and each could be contributing.
  • Corrected calcium — already on any metabolic panel. Catches a curable parathyroid problem that gets missed for decades.
  • Stool: calprotectin + a GI infection panel — calprotectin alone rules three things in or out for about $30.
This week · costs nothing

Four things to start before any appointment

A diary with clock times

Not "after food." Write "meal 1:10pm, joint pain 3:40pm."

Then check one thing: does her joint pain track time since eating, or just time of day? Meals cluster in the afternoon — and so does being tired and sore. If it is really just the afternoon, a whole theory dies for free.

Blood pressure around meals

Sit five minutes, take a reading, eat, then read every 15 minutes for two hours. Three different days.

A drop of 20 points or more is a real, measurable finding — and it would explain her dizziness. Happens in about a quarter of healthy young adults. Costs a cuff.

Photograph every supplement

A year of supplements, never once written down. Several common ones cause her exact symptoms: magnesium causes loose stool, vitamin A causes headache, nausea and joint pain together.

A cause could be sitting in the cupboard.

Track her cycle alongside symptoms

Takes three months and cannot be reconstructed from memory, so it has to start now.

Do not ask "are your symptoms cyclical" — nobody knows that. Ask: in the two days before her period, is the pain worse, the same, or better than mid-month?

Never asked in ten years

Questions worth more than most tests

  • Does the urge to go to the bathroom come 15–30 minutes after starting a meal, no matter what the meal was? If yes, that is a normal reflex being overactive — not a food reaction. This one question could dissolve much of a decade of blaming food.
  • Can she eat chicken, turkey and egg yolk? If she reacts to those too, the whole cat-protein theory weakens badly.
  • Does she react to capsules but not plain tablets? If so, the thing she reacts to is the shell, not the medicines.
  • Does butter on its own cause anything? She has probably already run this experiment without noticing.
  • Dry eyes or dry mouth? The free question that opens up an autoimmune possibility.
  • Straining, or feeling not-quite-finished in the bathroom? Points at a pelvic floor problem — common, missed, and treatable.
Just as important

Tests to refuse

Her actual problem is ten years of accumulated wrong labels. Each of these comes back "positive" far more often than the thing it claims to find — so each one risks manufacturing label number thirteen.

  • ✕ SIBO breath test. Under 5% chance it is the real answer. 30–40% chance it says "positive." That gap is the whole problem.
  • ✕ IgG or IgG4 food sensitivity panels, ALCAT, MRT. These measure exposure and tolerance, not disease. They are the main engine of unnecessary food restriction.
  • ✕ Serum DAO / histamine tests. Not a valid way to diagnose histamine intolerance.
  • ✕ "Leaky gut" testing, hair mineral analysis, urine mycotoxin panels.
  • ✕ Repeating the alpha-gal test. Two undetectable results a year apart. It is settled.
  • ✕ Genetic tryptase testing before the cheap blood test. Wrong order — the $50 one is the rule-out.
Honesty

Where we were wrong along the way

This page went through six rounds of review, and several confident earlier claims did not survive. Keeping the record visible matters more than looking consistent.

  • We built a first version on the wrong number. It assumed her alpha-gal IgE was high. It is undetectable. That version was discarded entirely.
  • We said "just cook it well done." Too optimistic — in the one proper study, 4 of 10 patients still reacted to heated protein.
  • We said "she has never eaten pork" was near-proof. It is not. Shared proteins in beef or lamb explain a pork result without any pork. What the number really shows is the ranking, not the exposure.
  • We ranked bile acid diarrhea first, from a statistic rather than from her. Her symptom pattern does not fit it. Dropped from first to eighth.
  • We over-warned about gelatin capsules. Gelatin contains no albumin. The real one to watch is porcine pancreatin — a crude tissue extract that may well be offered during a stomach workup.
  • We wrote a five-figure test list, then no treatment at all. Ten years of illness and not one suggestion of something to actually try. It is now a $150 panel — and worth asking about amitriptyline 10mg at night, which treats migraine, gut pain and sleep with one cheap tablet.
What never changed

Through every one of those revisions, the same two items stayed at the top: get epinephrine, and get the celiac test before stopping bread. That they survived every round of criticism is the strongest signal on this page.

This is research to discuss with her doctors — not a diagnosis, and not medical advice. Every test named here has to be ordered and interpreted by a physician.
