**Compiled August 2026.** A research dossier built around one specific patient profile, intended to be
read by the patient and handed to a treating allergist. Every substantive claim is sourced. Evidence
quality is graded honestly throughout, including where it is weak.

> **This is research, not a prescription.** Nothing here should be started, stopped, or changed
> without the treating physician. Its purpose is to make the next appointment dramatically more
> productive than the last one.

## 1. The honest answer about a "cure"

There is **no drug that cures alpha-gal syndrome**, and as of August 2026 there is **no FDA-approved
therapy for AGS specifically** and **not a single randomized controlled trial of any drug in this
disease**. Anyone selling certainty is selling something.

But AGS is genuinely different from every other serious food allergy, and the difference is the whole
story:

**Peanut allergy is usually for life. Alpha-gal syndrome frequently goes away.**

Alpha-gal IgE is not a permanent setting. It is an actively maintained antibody response that decays
once the thing driving it stops. In the University of Virginia longitudinal cohort, alpha-gal IgE fell
at a **median rate of ~53% per year** — roughly halving annually — in people who stopped getting tick
bites. **89% of patients who successfully avoided tick bites showed declining titers.** About **12% of
patients followed beyond five years became fully seronegative and returned to eating mammalian meat.**

And in November 2025, a Washington University group published what is effectively the first mapped
route to the other side: of 263 AGS patients, 21 met criteria for reintroduction, 19 chose to proceed,
and — following a graded 9-day at-home protocol stepping up through lean and then fatty beef —
**19 of 19 succeeded and fully liberalized their diets, with no relapses.**

So the accurate framing is not "there is no cure." It is:

> **Remission is a real, documented, reproducible endpoint in this disease. It is reached by
> subtraction rather than addition — by removing the driver and letting the immune system forget.
> The medical question is not "what cures it" but "how do we protect the decay, measure it, and stay
> functional during the years it takes."**

That is an answerable question, and the rest of this document answers it.

## 2. The clinical formulation for this patient

Four facts define the strategy:

| Fact | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Alpha-gal IgE > 20 kU/L** | Strongly sensitized. A long decay runway — but **this number does not predict reaction severity** |
| **GI-predominant symptoms + head pain** | The most misdiagnosed phenotype, and it has a *different* treatment approach than skin-predominant AGS |
| **Low-tick area / has moved away** | **The single most favorable prognostic factor in this disease.** The clock driving the illness has likely already stopped |
| **Reacts to dairy, gelatin, *and* medications** | A high-sensitivity pattern that matches a specific, named, treatable subtype — see below |

### The finding that matters most

In a Washington University cohort of 260 AGS patients, **34.3% (91 patients) had a distinct
comorbidity the authors termed *mast cell hyperactivity* (MCH)** — defined as patients who get only
**partial relief from dietary elimination** and continue to have episodic itching, hives, flushing,
abdominal pain, and headache.

The MCH subgroup was distinguished by exactly the features in this profile:

- **Much less likely to tolerate dairy: 34.4% vs 69.1%**
- **Much less likely to tolerate gelatin: 13.8% vs 42.2%**
- **Far higher rate of migraine: 21% vs 4%**
- Higher rates of GERD and other atopic conditions

The authors' conclusion is the operative sentence: *MCH patients have a unique profile and
comorbidities that **may warrant additional interventions beyond alpha-gal avoidance***.

**This is the most clinically actionable finding in this entire dossier.** Dairy reactivity plus
gelatin reactivity plus headache plus GI-predominant symptoms is not a coincidental cluster — it is a
described phenotype with a described treatment implication. It means:

> **If she is still suffering while genuinely avoiding mammalian products, the answer is probably not
> a stricter diet. It is mast-cell-directed treatment that has never been tried.**

One important nuance, stated honestly: the MCH group in that cohort had **lower** median alpha-gal IgE
(1.9 vs 3.8 kU/L), while her level is high. The most likely reading is that she has **both** a
high-titer classical AGS *and* a mast cell hyperactivity component layered on top. That combination
argues for treating both — the antibody with time and tick avoidance, the mast cells with drugs.

### One number that should be reassuring

A high alpha-gal IgE is frightening to look at, and it is widely misread. Across multiple studies,
**alpha-gal IgE titers do not predict the severity of reactions**. In 261 children and adults, IgE and
IgG titers were "not a predictor of the symptoms, the severity of reactions, or the time taken to
respond after eating meat." A 2025 analysis specifically looking for a correlation between alpha-gal
IgE and severity of the initial reaction found none.

What *does* predict severity: reaction history, cofactors (alcohol, exercise, NSAIDs, illness), asthma,
and comorbid mast cell disease or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia. **Those are the things to assess and
manage — not the titer.** The titer's job is to track the decay, and nothing else.

## 3. The central reframe: two clocks, not one

This is the idea that reorganizes everything, and it is the thing most commonly gotten wrong by
patients and physicians alike.

**There are two independent processes in alpha-gal syndrome, and they respond to completely different
interventions.**

### Clock 1 — The sensitization clock (determines whether she is ever cured)

Driven by **tick bites, and essentially only tick bites.** Prospective follow-up after tick bites
showed **≥20-fold increases in alpha-gal IgE**. Thomas Platts-Mills, who co-discovered the disease,
states it directly in a 2025 *Immunological Reviews* paper:

> *"The management of AGS symptoms is based on the avoidance of food derived from mammals; however,
> **the only thing that can allow IgE to decrease is avoidance of tick bites.**"*

And, critically: **eating mammalian meat does not boost alpha-gal IgE.** The strongest natural
experiment supporting this is the asymptomatic sensitized population — in a US screening-colonoscopy
cohort, **31.4% had alpha-gal IgE above 0.1 kU/L**, they were eating meat normally, and they showed no
progressive escalation and no excess symptoms.

### Clock 2 — The symptom clock (determines how she feels this week)

Driven by **exposure and cofactors.** Dietary alpha-gal, hidden exposures in medication, alcohol,
exercise, NSAIDs, illness, and the mast cell hyperactivity described above. This is what makes today
good or bad. It has **no effect on whether she is cured**.

### Why conflating them causes harm

Patients who believe diet drives the antibody do two damaging things: they restrict far beyond what
symptom control requires — shrinking their lives for no immunological benefit — and they become
demoralized when years of rigorous avoidance don't produce remission, concluding the disease is
hopeless when in fact they were never working on the right variable.

Separating the clocks yields a clean strategy:

- **For the cure:** be absolutely fanatical about tick bites. Nothing else on this list matters as much.
- **For symptoms:** be strategic and evidence-led, not maximally restrictive. Treat the mast cells.
- **To know which clock you are on:** measure the antibody on a schedule.

**A practical corollary worth stating plainly:** if her alpha-gal IgE is flat or rising despite
scrupulous dietary avoidance, the evidence points **first to unrecognized tick exposure** — not to a
missed food. Hunting for hidden dietary traces is the right response to ongoing *symptoms*. It is the
wrong response to a *non-declining antibody*.

## 4. The documented resolution pathway

### The eligibility criteria (from the 19/19 reintroduction study)

1. **Alpha-gal IgE declined to < 0.35 kU/L**, and
2. **No reactions while practicing mammalian avoidance**

Patients in that cohort had a mean alpha-gal IgE **at diagnosis** of 2.0 kU/L (range 0.16–20.3) and
attempted reintroduction an average of **3 years** after symptom onset (range 0.5–8 years). The
protocol itself is a **9-day graded at-home schedule** with incremental increases in both **quantity
and fat content** of beef — lean first, fatty later. That ordering is not arbitrary; see the
mechanism note below.

An earlier report from the same line of work: of 201 AGS patients, **14 (7%) met criteria**, 12 elected
to reintroduce, and the protocol proved feasible and safe.

### What this realistically means for her

Being honest about the arithmetic rather than optimistic: at a **median 53%/year decay** and starting
above 20 kU/L, reaching the 0.35 kU/L threshold takes roughly:

| Starting level | Approximate time to < 0.35 kU/L at median decay |
| --- | --- |
| 20 kU/L | ~5.5 years |
| 30 kU/L | ~6 years |
| 50 kU/L | ~6.5 years |

Two honest caveats in each direction. **Downward:** she is above the range of anyone in the published
reintroduction cohort, so she is not a near-term candidate and should not be led to expect one. About
**11% of tick-avoiders do not show decline at all.** **Upward:** decay rates vary widely between
individuals, the 53% figure is a median rather than a rule, some patients fall much faster, and if she
was diagnosed some years ago she may already be well into this curve — which is precisely why the
first action item is measuring it.

**Also worth knowing:** in the omalizumab cohort described below, *a few patients on treatment were
able to reintroduce small amounts of mammalian meat* before reaching the seroreversion threshold. So
"functional tolerance" and "serological remission" are not necessarily the same finish line, and the
first may arrive years before the second.

### Do not attempt reintroduction on a falling titer alone

This needs to be stated flatly because it is the way this goes wrong. The pathway requires
near-seroreversion **plus** a prolonged symptom-free interval **plus** medical supervision. A number
moving in the right direction is not permission. Roughly **15–20% of AGS patients undergoing
supervised food challenge require epinephrine and/or EMS transport** — which is exactly why
unsupervised experimentation is dangerous, and why the 19/19 result depended on the eligibility gate
rather than on the protocol.

### A mechanism worth understanding, because it is immediately useful

Alpha-gal rides on both glycoproteins and glycolipids, and it is **heat-stable** — cooking does not
destroy it. Work by Román-Carrasco and colleagues showed that **only alpha-gal bound to lipids crosses
the intestinal lining**, is packaged into chylomicrons, and then activates basophils. Dietary lipids
take 4–5 hours to reach the circulation, versus 1–2 hours for peptides.

This single finding explains two things at once:

- **Why reactions are delayed 3–6 hours.** The allergen arrives on the timetable of fat absorption.
- **Why fat content predicts risk.** This is directly actionable. In a 2025 *Allergy* study ranking
  allergenic potency across mammalian foods, the order ran: **organ meats (pork and beef kidney)
  highest → lean meats, liver, bacon → processed products → milk lowest.**

For someone with dairy reactivity, that last point matters a great deal for quality of life:
**full-fat dairy (ice cream, heavy cream, cream cheese, butter) is the high-risk category, while skim
milk and low-fat cheeses are the most likely to be tolerated.** Milk ranked lowest of all mammalian
foods tested. That is a much more livable rule than "no dairy," and it is mechanistically grounded
rather than folkloric — though any testing of the boundary should be done deliberately and with the
allergist, not opportunistically.

**A related warning that follows from the same mechanism:** organ meats produce **shorter-latency
reactions (under 1–2 hours)** and more consistent reactions than muscle meat. A reaction that comes on
in an hour does *not* rule out alpha-gal.

## 5. Track A — Protect the cure: tick-bite prevention as therapy

**This is the highest-value section in the document, and it is the one most likely to be skimmed.**

Reframe required: for this disease, tick prevention is not hygiene advice. **It is the only
disease-modifying therapy that exists.** A single new bite can reset years of progress and convert a
resolving patient back to symptomatic. Everything else in this dossier manages symptoms; this is the
only thing that changes the outcome.

She has already done the single biggest thing available — leaving a high-tick area. The job now is
making sure nothing quietly undoes it.

### The measures that have actual evidence

- **Permethrin-treated clothing.** In field studies of outdoor workers, factory-treated clothing
  **reduced tick bites by 65% in year one and 50% in year two (58% over two years)**. After three
  months of wear, **78% of pant swatches and 88% of sock swatches still produced ≥85% tick knockdown**.
  Note: **commercial/factory treatment substantially outperforms home spraying.** Apply to clothing,
  boots, and gear — **never to skin or underclothing.**
- **EPA-registered repellent** on exposed skin.
- **Systematic tick checks** after any outdoor exposure, and prompt removal.

### The exposure routes most likely to be missed

Because she has moved away, the risk is no longer ambient — it is episodic and therefore easy to
overlook:

1. **Travel back to endemic areas.** Visiting family in tick country is the classic silent
   re-sensitization event. Treat every such trip as a medical procedure requiring preparation.
2. **Pets.** Dogs that go outdoors carry lone star ticks indoors. This is a genuinely common and
   under-appreciated route. Veterinary tick prevention for the animals is part of *her* treatment plan.
3. **The yard, gardening, and yard-waste handling**, even in lower-prevalence regions — lone star tick
   range is expanding.
4. **Nymphal ticks**, which are poppy-seed sized and frequently never noticed. **A remembered bite is
   not required for sensitization**, so "I haven't been bitten" is not evidence of no exposure.

### The rule to hold onto

> **If the antibody stops falling, look for a tick — not for a food.**

And after any known bite: **re-check alpha-gal IgE about 2–3 months later**, because that is when a
boost would show up and it changes the whole trajectory.

## 6. Track B — Treat the mast cell component, not just the diet

Given the mast cell hyperactivity phenotype described in §2, this is where the near-term quality of
life is most likely to be won.

**Framing, stated honestly up front:** there are **no published observational studies or randomized
trials of any medicine for symptom control in AGS**. Everything below is off-label and extrapolated
from chronic urticaria, mastocytosis, and general food allergy. That does not make it unreasonable —
it makes it something to trial deliberately, with a defined endpoint and a defined stop date, rather
than accumulate indefinitely.

### 6.1 Oral cromolyn sodium (Gastrocrom) — the most mechanistically apt drug for the GI phenotype, and the most underused

This is the single most specific suggestion in this document for her presentation.

- **What it is:** an oral mast cell stabilizer, supplied as a 100 mg/5 mL oral concentrate.
- **Why it fits this phenotype precisely:** **less than 1% is absorbed.** It works *topically on the
  gut lining* — which makes it mechanistically well-matched to GI-predominant symptoms, and
  systemically very safe. The same property means it should **not** be expected to prevent systemic
  anaphylaxis.
- **FDA-approved for mastocytosis; use in AGS is off-label.**
- **Typical adult dosing:** 200 mg (two ampules) four times daily, 30 minutes before meals and at
  bedtime, not exceeding 40 mg/kg/day. Mixed in water — not juice, milk, or food.
- **Evidence in mastocytosis:** improvement in diarrhea, flushing, **headache**, vomiting, urticaria,
  abdominal pain, nausea and pruritus, with **onset over 2–6 weeks**.
- **The practical failure mode:** four-times-daily pre-meal dosing is a real adherence burden, and
  people abandon it at two weeks and call it a failure. **It needs a genuine 4–6 week trial** before
  being judged.

Note that headache appears explicitly in the mastocytosis symptom-response list — directly relevant to
her head pain.

### 6.2 Antihistamines, used properly

- **Second-generation H1 blockers**, up-dosed. The urticaria guidelines' second step is
  **up-dosing a single agent to as much as 4× the standard dose** (Grade A for cetirizine,
  levocetirizine, fexofenadine, bilastine).
- **Do not combine two different second-generation antihistamines** — guidelines advise against it.
  Up-dose one instead.
- Prefer the **long-acting agents (fexofenadine, levocetirizine — effective 22–28 hours)**, and
  consider bedtime dosing given the nocturnal reaction risk discussed in §8.
- **H2 blocker (famotidine 20–40 mg twice daily)** as a cheap, low-risk adjunct.

> **The most important safety sentence in this section: antihistamines do not treat anaphylaxis.**
> Their most dangerous property is making a developing anaphylaxis *feel* managed, which delays
> epinephrine. Epinephrine first, always.

### 6.3 Ketotifen

An H1 antagonist that also inhibits mast cell mediator release. **Not commercially available orally in
the US** — compounding pharmacy only. Typical dosing **1 mg twice daily, escalating to 2 mg twice
daily**. Main side effects sedation and weight gain. Evidence is thin.

Notably, an active UNC trial led by Scott Commins (**NCT07526558**, "Mast Cell Treatment in Post-tick
Bite Illness") is testing precisely the combination that fits this phenotype: **ketotifen 1→2 mg BID +
cromolyn 200 mg TID + fexofenadine**. This is the first genuinely AGS-relevant interventional
treatment trial in existence, and it is aimed at exactly her problem.

### 6.4 Montelukast — include the warning

Sometimes added after antihistamine up-dosing fails. **Carries an FDA boxed warning (2020) for serious
neuropsychiatric events** — agitation, depression, suicidal ideation. In a patient already carrying
anxiety about unpredictable nocturnal reactions, this deserves explicit discussion rather than a quiet
prescription.

### 6.5 Omalizumab (Xolair) — the strongest option, and the most strategically interesting

**Mechanism.** An anti-IgE antibody. It reduces **free IgE by more than 95% within days** and
**downregulates FcεRI receptors on basophils by ~73% within 7 days**, rendering mast cells and
basophils substantially less responsive to allergen. It also reduces FcεRI on dendritic cells, which
may dampen allergen presentation and Th2 activation.

**The AGS-specific evidence — the best that exists, which is still not strong:**

| Study | n | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retrospective cohort, Apr 2022–Aug 2025 | **58** | **50/58 (86%) reported reduced frequency or severity of reactions; zero epinephrine use for food events.** 41/58 (71%) still on therapy at median 16 months. **7 of 10 patients with mast-cell-associated symptoms improved.** Joint pain in 4/58 |
| Earlier case series | 14 | UAS7 (urticaria score) fell 23.3 → 4.2 at 4 weeks → 0.4 at 12 weeks; 12/14 improved tolerance of accidental exposure |

**Why it is especially interesting for her:** the 7-of-10 improvement in *mast-cell-associated
symptoms* speaks directly to the MCH phenotype, and **a few patients on omalizumab were able to
reintroduce small amounts of mammalian meat** — before reaching seroreversion.

That suggests the most powerful strategy available to her:

> **Use omalizumab as a bridge. Let it restore function and reduce symptom burden now, while the
> antibody decays naturally over the following years on the tick-avoidance track. Then, once the titer
> approaches threshold, taper and attempt the graded reintroduction protocol.**

This is a two-track plan in which the drug buys quality of life during exactly the years the immune
system needs to forget — rather than treating the wait as time to simply endure.

**Regulatory status.** Omalizumab was **FDA-approved on 16 February 2024 for IgE-mediated food
allergy** (age ≥1), dosed **75–600 mg every 2 or 4 weeks by total IgE and body weight**. AGS *is* an
IgE-mediated food allergy, so it arguably falls within the literal indication — but the pivotal
OUtMATCH trial studied peanut, milk, egg, cashew and wheat, all **protein** allergens. **No
carbohydrate allergen was studied.** Practically: this is a label argument, not a data argument, and
approval will turn on the payer's prior-authorization language. Expect friction; document failure of
avoidance.

> ### ⚠️ A safety point that is easy to miss and genuinely important
>
> **Omalizumab is manufactured in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, which variably glycosylate
> proteins with alpha-gal.** This is the same mechanism that causes cetuximab anaphylaxis. Baseline
> anaphylaxis risk to omalizumab is ~0.1% in asthma, and the published AGS literature explicitly warns
> the risk **may be higher in AGS patients.**
>
> **The first dose should be given with full anaphylaxis readiness.** Xolair also carries a boxed
> warning for anaphylaxis. This is not a routine biologic in this population — but it is a manageable
> risk, not a reason to decline.

**Practical requirements:** draw **baseline total IgE before the first dose** — it is uninterpretable
afterward, and it determines dosing. Patients whose total IgE exceeds the dosing table's ceiling fall
off-protocol. Cost is roughly $30,000–60,000/year at list; the manufacturer co-pay program covers up to
$15,000/year for commercially insured patients, with a foundation pathway for the uninsured.

**Honest evidence grade: weak-to-moderate.** Uncontrolled, single-center, patient-reported outcomes, no
placebo, no food-challenge confirmation, 16-month follow-up — in a disease that improves on its own at
53%/year. The authors themselves call for prospective studies. It is the best available option, and
the confidence interval around it is wide.

## 7. Track C — The medication and exposure audit

**Flagged as urgent because she reacts to medications.** This is where a serious, preventable reaction
is most likely to be hiding, and it is the part almost no general allergist does systematically.

### 7.1 The highest-priority items to check right now

**Conjugated equine estrogens — Premarin, Prempro.** Derived from **pregnant mare urine** — a
mammalian source — and specifically identified as a medication for AGS patients to avoid. If she is on
hormone therapy containing conjugated estrogens, **this is a daily alpha-gal exposure that will never
appear on any food diary**, and it could single-handedly explain persistent symptoms despite a clean
diet. Synthetic and plant-derived estradiol alternatives exist. **Check the bottle.**

**Desiccated thyroid extract — Armour Thyroid, NP Thyroid, Nature-Throid.** These are porcine thyroid
gland. This matters more than its obscurity suggests: in a 2026 *Allergy* study measuring allergenic
potency by basophil activation, **thyroglobulin was the single most potent alpha-gal allergen tested —
active below 10 pg/mL in every patient**, ahead of pancreatic enzymes and pork kidney. **Synthetic
levothyroxine is the preferred alternative in AGS.** If she takes desiccated thyroid, this is arguably
the most urgent single item in this document.

**Gelatin capsules.** Described as the excipient of greatest concern, and directly relevant given her
gelatin reactivity. **HPMC (plant-derived) capsules** are the substitute — available through
compounding or by finding an alternative manufacturer. A documented AGS reaction has been traced to a
gelatin capsule.

**Pancreatic enzyme replacement (Creon, Zenpep, Viokase, Pertzye).** Porcine pancreas, ranked among the
most potent alpha-gal products tested. Important nuance: **reactivity is brand-specific and skin-test
positivity does not equal clinical reactivity** — two patients tolerated porcine enzymes despite
positive skin tests, with one negative to Creon but positive to three others. If she needs these, it
warrants allergist-supervised testing rather than blanket avoidance.

**Heparin (porcine).** See §8 — the most important surgical risk.

### 7.2 Items commonly on "avoid" lists that the evidence does **not** support

Being accurate here matters, because over-restriction has real costs and crowds out attention from the
things that are genuinely dangerous.

- **Magnesium stearate / stearic acid — WEAK/DISPUTED.** Not structurally similar to alpha-gal; any
  risk would be residual contamination. **As of 2022 the major commercial sources are plant-based
  (coconut and palm).** Provider guidance states tablets containing magnesium stearate or lactose
  contain "little to no alpha-gal and are tolerated by most people." Worth investigating in a
  genuinely refractory patient; not a routine avoid.
- **Carrageenan — DISPUTED, and genuinely unsettled.** A 2015 *JACI* analysis concluded most AGS
  patients should tolerate it and strict avoidance was not required. A 2025 *JACI* paper found it the
  **second most commonly self-reported allergen at 10.9%** of AGS patients — but that is *self-report*,
  not challenge-confirmed, and 2026 basophil testing found carrageenan showed **low** allergenicity.
  Other clinical summaries put the real rate at 1–2%. **Reasonable position:** not a routine avoid, but
  a legitimate targeted elimination trial for someone with persistent unexplained reactions —
  particularly medication reactions, which is her situation.
- **Personal care products (lanolin, tallow soaps, collagen skincare) — WEAK.** **No published study
  demonstrates transdermal absorption of alpha-gal sufficient to cause systemic reactions.** Advocacy
  guidance itself says most AGS patients tolerate lanolin and do not need to change personal care
  products. Reserve for genuinely refractory patients after diet and medications are cleared.
- **Airborne exposure from cooking — WEAK but widely reported.** Mechanistically plausible (heat-stable
  allergen, aerosolized fat) and reported by perhaps 10–30% of patients by informal expert estimate,
  but **no blinded challenge has ever been published.**

### 7.3 Cofactors — the reason the same food is fine on Tuesday and dangerous on Saturday

Cofactors lower the reaction threshold, and they explain the maddening inconsistency that makes
patients doubt their own diagnosis. The main ones: **alcohol, exercise, NSAIDs, intercurrent illness,
stress, poor sleep, and high-fat meals.** Proposed mechanisms include increased intestinal
permeability (exercise, NSAIDs), relaxed gut epithelium (alcohol), and increased splanchnic
circulation.

**Practical rule: avoid alcohol, vigorous exercise, and NSAIDs in the hours surrounding any possible
exposure.** Prefer acetaminophen over NSAIDs. Cofactor stacking is what converts a tolerated exposure
into a reaction.

## 8. The safety file: what could actually cause serious harm

This section exists because the realistic worst-case outcomes in AGS are not dietary slip-ups. They are
medical procedures, and they are preventable with a phone call in advance.

### 8.1 Surgery, anesthesia, and procedures — prepare weeks ahead, not the morning of

Recommended actions:

1. **Get "alpha-gal" entered into the EMR as an allergy**, which triggers allergy banding and flags the
   anesthesia team.
2. **Request a pre-surgical consult with both surgeon and anesthesiologist weeks in advance.**
3. **Ask a pharmacist to review every intraoperative agent and every discharge prescription** —
   including capsule shells.
4. **Name specific products**, because "no mammal products" is too abstract to be actionable:
   - **Heparin** for cardiopulmonary bypass. In 8,819 cardiac surgery patients, 17 had positive
     pre-op alpha-gal tests and **4 of 17 (24%) had an allergic reaction — rising to 50% among those
     with recent IgE levels.** Median titer in reactors was **75 IU/mL vs 8 in non-reactors.**
     Pre-screening and premedication are recommended, and **desensitization protocols exist.**
   - **Gelatin hemostatic agents:** Gelfoam, Surgifoam, Surgiflo (porcine), FloSeal (bovine).
     Documented intraoperative anaphylaxis. In dentistry, **4 of 5 documented AGS reactions involved
     hemostatic agents.**
   - **Gelatin colloid plasma expanders:** Gelofusine, Gelaspan, Haemaccel. IV route amplifies risk
     even where intrinsic potency is low.
   - Bovine/porcine heart valves and patches, collagen dressings and dural substitutes, catgut suture,
     biologic mesh, xenograft bone, **glycerin-containing propofol formulations**, lidocaine patches.
5. **Request documented substitutes:** oxidized regenerated cellulose (Surgicel), plant-based
   polysaccharide hemostats, **human recombinant** thrombin, synthetic sealants, HPMC capsules,
   synthetic sutures, crystalloid or albumin instead of gelatin colloid. One neurosurgical review
   audited 25 commonly used agents and found **64% potentially unsafe for AGS patients** — with safe
   alternatives available for essentially all of them.

### 8.2 Transfusion — a newly recognized risk worth knowing her blood type for

**Transfusion-Related Alpha-Gal Syndrome (TRAGS).** The alpha-gal antigen closely resembles the ABO
blood group B antigen. **Group O recipients** receiving **group B or AB plasma or platelets** can have
severe allergic transfusion reactions. At least **5 severe cases are documented, including one death**,
some in patients with no history of meat allergy. A multinational analysis of over 550,000 transfusions
found group O patients received group B products in ~3–4% of transfusions — so the exposure is not
rare. A nationwide analysis published in *JAMA Internal Medicine* in August 2026 concluded TRAGS is a
legitimate manifestation of AGS that transfusion services should account for.

**Action: know her ABO group.** If she is group O, that belongs in her medical record alongside the
alpha-gal diagnosis, with a note to avoid group B and AB plasma and platelets where feasible.

### 8.3 The delayed-reaction problem and nocturnal risk

Dinner at 7pm plus a 3–6 hour delay means symptom onset between **10pm and 1am** — asleep, unobserved,
with the earliest symptoms (itching, cramping) easily slept through. This is the highest-mortality-risk
structural feature of the disease. A **fatal anaphylaxis four hours after eating beef** is documented,
and the first reported AGS death was described in late 2025.

Mitigation:

1. **Eat unfamiliar or higher-risk meals early in the day**, so the reaction window falls during waking
   hours with people around.
2. **Never eat a high-risk meal alone before sleeping.** Tell someone what was eaten and when.
3. **Keep epinephrine on the bedside table** — not in a bag downstairs. Two doses.
4. **Suppress cofactors** around any possible exposure.
5. **Keep a timestamped food-and-symptom diary** recording both ingestion time and onset time. This is
   the only reliable way to establish her personal latency and identify cofactor patterns — and for a
   GI-predominant patient it is the single most useful diagnostic instrument available.

### 8.4 Epinephrine, and the AGS-specific action plan problem

**Carry two doses at all times.** Options now include standard autoinjectors and **neffy nasal spray**
(2 mg for ≥30 kg, approved Aug 2024; 1 mg for 15–30 kg, approved 2025) — a genuine advance for
needle-averse patients, though approved on pharmacokinetic bridging rather than clinical anaphylaxis
outcome trials.

> **The critical customization:** generic food-allergy action plans are built around immediate
> oral and respiratory symptoms. For a GI-predominant patient they are actively misleading. Her plan
> must **explicitly name severe abdominal pain, repetitive vomiting, and faintness as
> epinephrine-level symptoms**, because those are the symptoms she will actually have.

**What emergency departments get wrong:** the observation clock runs from **symptom onset**, not from
ingestion. An AGS patient arriving five hours after eating is *at* the peak of allergen presentation,
not past it. **Observation should not be shortened because "the meal was hours ago."** Standard is
4–6 hours after epinephrine, extending to 4–12 with risk factors.

**On corticosteroids:** they have no immediate effect in anaphylaxis and the current practice
parameter does **not** support routine use to prevent biphasic reactions. The reflexive steroid taper
after every reaction is not evidence-based.

## 9. Tests to request, and how to read them

### The core order

**Labcorp test 650003, the Alpha-Gal IgE Panel** — this is the better order than alpha-gal IgE alone
(650001), because it bundles **total IgE plus alpha-gal, beef, pork, and lamb IgE** in a single draw.
Total IgE is needed for the ratio below and for omalizumab dosing.

### Reading the numbers

| Level (kU/L) | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| < 0.10 | Negative — the seroreversion target |
| **0.10–0.34** | The CDC/CSTE diagnostic threshold when paired with compatible history |
| **< 0.35** | **The reintroduction eligibility threshold** |
| 0.35–2.0 | Conventional positive |
| ≥ 2.0 | Diagnosis very likely when history fits |
| > 20 | Strongly sensitized — **but this does not predict severity** |

### The alpha-gal : total IgE ratio — useful, but for a different purpose than commonly claimed

A widespread claim is that this ratio predicts *severity*. **It does not, and neither does the absolute
level.** What the ratio predicts is whether sensitization is **clinically real** versus incidental.

From the primary dataset (Mabelane et al., with all patients taken to oral food challenge): the best
overall classification came at **sIgE 2.00 kU/L and a ratio of 0.75%**, and **95% probability of
challenge-proven allergy at sIgE > 5.5 kU/L and a ratio of 2.12%**. The commonly quoted rule of thumb
is **≥2 kU/L or >2% of total IgE**. In symptomatic AGS the alpha-gal fraction is often **10–50% of
total IgE**, which is extraordinary for a single food component.

The ratio earns its place in two failure modes of the raw number: the **non-atopic patient with low
total IgE**, where a modest absolute value understates real disease; and the **highly atopic patient**,
where a decent-looking absolute value may be a bystander finding.

### Additional tests worth requesting

- **Baseline serum tryptase**, drawn at clinical baseline (not during or shortly after a reaction).
- **TPSAB1 copy number by droplet digital PCR** *if baseline tryptase is ≥ 8 ng/mL* — testing for
  **hereditary alpha-tryptasemia (HaT)**. This is worth doing and rarely done. HaT affects **4–6% of
  the general population**, accounts for **64–72% of all elevated baseline tryptase**, and
  **independently increases both the incidence and severity of anaphylaxis** with a gene-dosage effect.
  The optimal tryptase cutoff to predict it is **9.2 ng/mL (sensitivity 98.1%, specificity 96.6%)**.
  Available through ARUP Laboratories. There is no HaT-specific therapy, but a positive result
  justifies carrying two epinephrine devices, a lower threshold to use them, more aggressive baseline
  mediator blockade, and family counseling — and it explains an elevated tryptase without committing
  her to a bone marrow biopsy.
- **KIT p.D816V** in peripheral blood if reactions are severe or unexplained, before settling on
  "idiopathic."

### Monitoring schedule

- **Alpha-gal IgE + total IgE every 6–12 months**, same lab and same platform every time — different
  assay platforms are not interchangeable, and serial comparison is the entire point.
- **Re-check 2–3 months after any known tick bite.**
- Record **tick exposure history at every single draw.** The antibody trend is meaningless without it.

*(Evidence note: the 6–12 month interval is expert and patient-organization practice, not a formal
guideline recommendation. The underlying decay-rate data is solid; the interval is convention.)*

### One test to decline

**Alpha-gal IgG or IgG4 testing has no diagnostic role.** Every human makes natural anti-alpha-gal
IgG — a positive result is normal physiology being sold as a diagnosis. The same applies to general
IgG food-sensitivity panels.

Three questions that separate informed from merely aware:

   phenotype.

   is *that's sensitization, not syndrome*. Up to **24–31% of people in high-burden states are
   seropositive** while only a small minority have clinical disease.

Bonus signal: an allergist who raises **cofactors** unprompted is reading the literature.

## 11. The pipeline: what is coming, and when

**Near-term, and genuinely promising — BTK inhibitors.** This is the most exciting real signal in the
field, and the reason is mechanistic: **BTK sits downstream of the IgE receptor and is
allergen-agnostic** — it does not care whether the epitope is a peanut protein or a carbohydrate.
Which means it *should* work in AGS, where drugs developed against protein allergens may not.

- **Remibrutinib** — already FDA-approved (Sept 2025) for chronic spontaneous urticaria, so the
  molecule exists and is prescribable. In Phase 2 for peanut allergy: **86.7% tolerating ≥600 mg
  peanut protein at the 100 mg BID dose vs 0% on placebo**, after just 4 weeks. **Phase 3 food allergy
  program planned for 2H 2026.**
- **Acalabrutinib** — in 10 adults with peanut allergy, baseline tolerance rose from a median of 29 mg
  to **7 of 10 tolerating the 4,044 mg maximum** after four doses. 2025 work suggests it can even
  **abort ongoing anaphylaxis** in animal models.

**Caveat:** n=59 and n=10, four weeks of dosing, and **zero AGS patients have ever received a BTK
inhibitor in a trial.** But if she is going to be waiting several years for antibody decay, this is the
class most likely to change her options during that window, and it is worth asking her allergist to
watch.

**Available now, and unusual — GalSafe pork.** Alpha-gal knockout pigs, **FDA-approved December 2020**.
It is **not sold commercially anywhere** — no agriculture partner would take it on. Instead,
**Revivicor gives it away free**: the herd lives in Iowa, meat is processed at a USDA-certified plant,
and frozen shipments are mailed to AGS patients who apply. Reportedly "hundreds and hundreds of
orders." Honest caveat: FDA found no detectable alpha-gal across generations but **did not formally
determine it is safe for AGS patients to eat**, so this remains unvalidated regulatory territory and
should be discussed with her allergist first.

**Longer-term:** tolerogenic nanoparticles carrying alpha-gal (mouse data only, and **prophylactic** —
given *before* sensitization, so not applicable to an already-sensitized person); next-generation
anti-IgE molecules engineered to actively strip IgE off mast cell receptors (mouse); anti-tick vaccines
(preclinical, 5–10 years out, and currently built against *Ixodes* rather than the lone star tick that
drives most US cases).

**Policy worth tracking:** **H.R. 1178, the Alpha-gal Allergen Inclusion Act**, would make alpha-gal
the 10th labeled major food allergen. It reached a House Health Subcommittee agenda in April 2026. If
it passes, the hidden-ingredient problem that dominates §7 becomes dramatically smaller.

## 12. What does not work, and what will be sold to you anyway

This section is included because desperation is a market, and AGS has a structural feature that makes
it unusually easy to exploit:

> **The disease remits on its own at roughly 53% per year. Any intervention followed by months of
> observation will appear to work. This makes AGS a near-perfect environment for false attribution of
> natural recovery to whatever the patient happened to be doing at the time.**

Hold every claimed cure to that standard, including the ones in this document.

- **SAAT / auricular acupuncture.** Heavily marketed to AGS patients with claimed **94.8–96% remission**
  from a retrospective review of 137 patients. It does not hold up: retrospective, uncontrolled,
  unblinded, **lab values unavailable for many patients**, many simply reintroduced meat without
  confirmatory testing, mechanism unknown, and hopelessly confounded by natural decay. **The decisive
  data point:** in the 58-patient omalizumab cohort, **9 patients had previously failed SAAT** before
  starting omalizumab. Patients who fail do not appear in acupuncture clinic case series. Most search
  results promoting it are clinic marketing pages.
- **"Treat the chronic Lyme and the alpha-gal will resolve."** No mechanism, no data. **AGS follows the
  tick bite, not the infection** — there is no correlation between IgE to *Ehrlichia* or *Rickettsia*
  and AGS. Prolonged antibiotic courses for "chronic Lyme" have failed in randomized trials and carry
  real harm (line infections, *C. difficile*, cholestasis). Screen for coinfections on their own
  merits; do not expect it to touch the AGS. *(The one genuine link runs the other direction:
  *Anaplasma* infection raises alpha-gal content **in the tick**, possibly increasing the dose
  delivered by a bite.)*
- **IgG / IgG4 food sensitivity panels.** Everyone makes natural anti-alpha-gal IgG. A positive result
  is physiology, not diagnosis.
- **Rituximab.** Mechanistically wrong — plasma cells express little CD20 and are spared, and a
  randomized trial found no significant change in serum IgE. Included only to close the question.
- **Dupilumab monotherapy.** Failed to induce tolerance in a peanut allergy trial. There are **zero AGS
  data**. The case report frequently cited as evidence was in a patient who tested **negative** for
  alpha-gal.
- **"Aspirin desensitization."** Appears on patient forums; it is a therapy for a completely different
  disease (NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease). **The direction of the relationship is the opposite
  of therapeutic — NSAIDs are a documented cofactor that worsens AGS reactions.**
- **Herbal detox protocols, ozone therapy, chelation, colloidal silver, parasite cleanses.** No
  evidence; several are directly harmful.
- **One to read carefully rather than dismiss:** a January 2026 *C&EN* article titled *"α-Gal causes
  tick-related meat allergies—it could also be therapeutic"* circulates in AGS communities and is
  routinely misread. It is about using alpha-gal to recruit immune cells for **wound healing and
  cancer immunotherapy**. **For an AGS patient those products are a hazard, not a treatment.**

## 13. The 30-day plan

Ordered by expected value.

**Week 1 — Establish the baseline**
1. **Order the Alpha-Gal IgE Panel (Labcorp 650003)** — alpha-gal IgE *and* total IgE together. Without
   a current number and a second one later, there is no way to know which clock she is on. If prior
   results exist, gather them with dates to plot the trend.
2. **Add baseline serum tryptase** to the same draw, at clinical baseline.
3. **Audit the medicine cabinet against §7.** Specifically and immediately: **conjugated estrogens
   (Premarin/Prempro), desiccated thyroid (Armour/NP Thyroid), gelatin capsules, pancreatic enzymes.**
   This is the highest-yield hour available in the entire plan, and it can be done today.
4. **Start the timestamped food-and-symptom diary** — ingestion time *and* onset time, plus cofactors
   (alcohol, exercise, NSAIDs, illness, sleep).

**Week 2 — Secure the safety floor**
5. **Confirm two in-date epinephrine devices**, one on the bedside table.
6. **Rewrite the action plan** so it names **severe abdominal pain, repetitive vomiting, and faintness**
   as epinephrine-level symptoms. Generic plans will not.
7. **Find out her ABO blood group** and get alpha-gal into the EMR as a formal allergy.
8. **Lock down tick prevention** — permethrin-treated clothing for any travel to endemic areas,
   veterinary tick prevention for any pets, and a standing rule to treat trips back to tick country as
   requiring preparation.

**Weeks 3–4 — Get the right specialist and open the treatment conversation**
9. **Request referral to UNC (Commins/McGill), UVA (Wilson), Vanderbilt (Stone), or WashU (Jerath).**
   Given a GI-predominant phenotype *plus* medication reactions, **McGill at UNC** and **Stone at
   Vanderbilt** map most precisely onto her two problems. Expect a wait; start now.
10. **Open two specific conversations with the allergist:**
    - **A 4–6 week trial of oral cromolyn** for the GI symptoms, with a defined endpoint — the most
      mechanistically apt and most underused option for this phenotype.
    - **Omalizumab candidacy**, including the CHO-cell alpha-gal first-dose precaution, and baseline
      total IgE drawn *before* the first dose.
11. **Send alphagaldocs.org (with its CME module) to the current physician** if they are willing but
    under-informed.

**Then, ongoing**
12. **Re-test alpha-gal IgE in 6–12 months, same lab.** Two points make a trend; the trend is the
    entire prognosis.

## 14. Questions to bring to the allergist

1. What is her current alpha-gal IgE, what was it before, and **what is the trend line?**
2. What is her **alpha-gal IgE as a percentage of total IgE?**
3. What is her **baseline tryptase** — and if it is ≥8 ng/mL, will you test **TPSAB1 copy number** for
   hereditary alpha-tryptasemia?
4. Given GI-predominant symptoms plus headache plus dairy and gelatin reactivity, **does she fit the
   mast cell hyperactivity phenotype, and does that change the treatment plan?**
5. Can we trial **oral cromolyn** for the GI symptoms, for a full 4–6 weeks before judging it?
6. **Is she a candidate for omalizumab?** It is FDA-approved for IgE-mediated food allergy. If yes,
   are you aware it is produced in CHO cells that can carry alpha-gal, and will the first dose be given
   with full anaphylaxis readiness?
7. Have we audited **every medication and supplement**, including capsule shells — and specifically
   conjugated estrogens and desiccated thyroid?
8. Does her action plan name **GI symptoms** as epinephrine-level? Does she have two devices?
9. What is our **tick-bite prevention plan**, and do you agree it is the only disease-modifying
   intervention available?
10. **What alpha-gal IgE level would make you consider supervised reintroduction, and what is our
    re-testing schedule between now and then?**

## Appendix: evidence grading used in this document

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **STRONG** | Replicated in peer-reviewed literature, quantified, or mechanistically demonstrated |
| **MODERATE** | Consistent observational data, case series, or in-vitro evidence; no controlled trial |
| **WEAK / DISPUTED** | Patient report, expert opinion, theoretical, or contradicted by other data |
| **UNPROVEN** | No supporting evidence; included to close the question |

**The overarching caveat, restated because it governs everything above:** there is no randomized
controlled trial of any drug in alpha-gal syndrome. Every efficacy figure quoted here comes from an
uncontrolled study in a disease that improves on its own at a median 53% per year. "86% improved" and
"19 of 19 tolerated" are real and meaningful results — and they are also both consistent with a
substantial contribution from natural history. The honest position is that these are the best
available options, held with appropriate uncertainty, and that the one intervention with an
unambiguous mechanism and an unambiguous effect on the disease's trajectory remains the simplest one:
**do not get bitten again.**
